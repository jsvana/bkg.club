A note on the register

If you arrived here from the rest of bkg.club, you may have noticed that this page is spelled correctly. That is deliberate, and it is the only concession we are making.

Everything else on this site is written the way we talk on the air at two in the morning, and we stand by it. But this page has a different job. This is the page you forward to a club president, paste into a newsletter, or read off a phone at the front of a Thursday night meeting — and "get ur brass on the air!!" does not survive that trip intact.

So: here is the Brass Knuckle Gang in plain language. What we are actually trying to do, why a club should care, and what it costs to find out. Roughly thirty dollars, as it turns out, which is less than your club spent on coffee last year.

The number one goal
GROW 2-METER CW

That is the entire mission. Not "grow the club," not "sell shirts," not "win anything." Put more operators on 144 MHz sending Morse code than there were last year.

Everything below this line is a second-order effect — the useful things that happen on the way to that goal. If you are a club looking for a reason to care about a gang of people with brass knuckles in their logo, the side effects are the reason.

⚡ THE THREE SECOND-ORDER EFFECTS ⚡

01
THE BAND GETS USED

Two meters is not what it was. Outside of a handful of repeaters and a net or two, large stretches of the band go days without a signal on them, and the weak-signal end goes weeks.

A band with activity on it is a great deal easier to defend than an empty one. Every CW contact is a small, verifiable argument that 144 MHz is still doing something worth doing — and it costs you nothing but an evening.

02
PEOPLE START TINKERING AGAIN

The barrier to modifying a radio is almost never skill. It is fear. Nobody's first soldering job should be inside a thirteen-hundred-dollar HF rig, and everyone knows it, so the iron stays in the drawer and the hobby quietly becomes shopping.

Nobody's hands shake over a thirty-dollar handheld. When the worst case is "I have to order another one," people try things. People who try things become people who build things. That is the whole trick.

03
IT DRAGS YOU BACK TO YOUR LOCAL CLUB

This is the one clubs tend to miss. With a handheld and a modest antenna, 2-meter CW is a local mode. Your contacts are not strangers a thousand miles away whom you will never meet. They are people who live close enough to have breakfast with.

Which means you cannot grow in this gang without meeting the hams around you. You have to go back to your local club, talk your friends into modifying a radio, and get on the air together. The recruiting mechanism is, by physics, community building.

The hardware

THE NR7Y MODS AND FIRMWARE

Here is the part that makes all of this possible, and the reason a club can run a build night instead of a lecture.

The Quansheng UV-K1 is an inexpensive Chinese handheld — call it thirty dollars, give or take, depending on the week and the seller. Out of the box it is an FM handheld and it will not send CW at all.

We point clubs at the K1 specifically, and the reason is predictability. The older UV-K5 shipped in several hardware revisions that are difficult to tell apart before you buy, and which firmware you need depends on which one turns up in the box. The K1 is one known target. Order five radios, get five identical radios, and the same instructions work on all of them — which matters enormously when you are standing in front of a room of people holding soldering irons.

NR7Y wrote the CW firmware that changes what the radio can do. On the K1 it builds on F4HWN's Fusion codebase — which descends from egzumer's custom firmware and, before that, DualTachyon's original open-source work — and it turns the radio into a genuine CW transceiver. Not a tone whistled into an FM microphone: real carrier keying, with transmit and receive switching fast enough to send and hear properly in real time.

What you get
Keying
True CW carrier keying, with high-speed RX/TX switching for real-time sending.
Keyer
Built-in automatic iambic keyer with mode A, mode B, Ultimatic and Bug, plus adjustable speed in words per minute.
Sidetone
Adjustable sidetone volume, including off, for the people who prefer to suffer.
The modification
Two resistors come off the board, two jumpers go on, and the custom firmware goes in. That is the entire job. A standard paddle then plugs straight into the radio — no adapter, no special cable, nothing to lose in a go-bag.
Messages
Record macro messages by keying them in, then play them back — your call, your exchange, a CQ you do not have to send by hand forty times during an activation.
Code practice
A built-in practice mode that does not transmit, so a new operator learns on the same radio they will later use on the air. It will even send with the flashlight LED.
Flashing
Prebuilt firmware files are published with every release — nothing to compile and no toolchain to install. Follow the current steps in the documentation, and back up the radio's calibration data with uvtools2 once you are done.
🤜 What the club gains
  • A working 2m CW rig for the price of a large pizza.
  • A build night with a real, finished result at the end of it.
  • An excuse to teach flashing, keying and antennas in one evening.
  • Members who have opened a radio and lived to tell about it.
⚠ What to know going in
  • The author's own warning, and it is a fair one: this mod changes the radio's hardware behavior in significant ways, with no guarantee against damaging it. Use at your own risk.
  • Back up the calibration data with uvtools2 right after flashing. Cheap insurance, good habit.
  • CHIRP needs the dedicated driver that ships alongside each firmware release. Download them together and save yourself the phone call.
  • Buy the K1, not the original UV-K5. This firmware does not run on the older radio, and that is the mistake most likely to cost a club an evening.

The playbook

RUNNING THIS AT YOUR CLUB

You do not need a committee, a budget line, or a vote. You need one evening and about a hundred dollars, which is to say three radios and a spare.

BUY THREE RADIOS, NOT THIRTY

Start small enough that failure is boring. Three UV-K1s and a programming cable is a rounding error in most club treasuries, and it is enough to prove the idea or kill it.

HOLD A FLASHING NIGHT

One laptop, one cable, an hour. Deliberately recruit the members who have never flashed anything in their lives — they are the entire audience for this exercise. Watching someone's face when their handheld reboots into something they changed is the actual product here.

TWO RESISTORS OFF, TWO JUMPERS ON

The hardware modification is genuinely small: lift two resistors off the board, add two jumpers, and the key input is live. Put the steadiest hands on the iron for the first one and let everybody else crowd around and watch before they try their own. This is the part the evening is actually about — somebody changing a circuit rather than a setting, probably for the first time.

GET ON THE AIR LOCALLY, AND GO SLOWLY

Pick a night, coordinate a simplex frequency in the CW portion of the band, and run it at a speed the newest operator can actually copy. QRS is a feature, not an apology. A 5 WPM contact counts exactly as much as a 25 WPM one.

SPONSOR THE NEW ONES IN

When a member works a non-member on 2m CW, that is a recruit. Confirm they actually want to join, then send in the contact — they get a BKG number and appear on the downline under whoever pounded them in. Clubs have a habit of turning that into a leaderboard. We are not going to stop you.

The fine print

THE GROWN-UP PARAGRAPH

We describe ourselves as a law-abiding, FCC-respecting gang, and we mean the second part as sincerely as we mean the first.

Operate inside your privileges and your band plan. In the United States, 2-meter CW lives at the weak-signal end of the band, with 144.100 MHz the traditional CW calling frequency and the segment below it spoken for by EME and other weak-signal work. Check your own national and regional band plan, coordinate locally, and do not park on top of somebody's moonbounce schedule.

Modifying a radio is your call and your consequence. The mod is well-trodden and the radios are cheap, but you own the outcome — including anything it does to warranty or certification. Modify your own radio, or one whose owner is standing next to you saying yes.

Membership is voluntary, every time. Confirm that someone actually wants to be in the gang before you submit them. A surprise membership is not a gift; it is paperwork.
